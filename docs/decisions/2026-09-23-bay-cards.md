# Decision brief: bay cards (daughter boards) and an 8-port brain

Date 2026-09-23. Owner: dmz. Question from the owner after the v3 routing result:
would daughter boards help, would they make 8 ports easy, and the board may be
resized. Open as D9.

## What the routing runs taught us

Three things hold the monolithic board back, and all three sit in the bay block:

1. The bridge QFNs (ASM1153E, 0.4 mm pitch) and their supply pins, four times.
2. The USB 3 pairs from the hubs to those four bridges (long, unmatched).
3. The four 28 mm-wide SATA receptacles that set the rear-edge width, which is
   why an 8-bay monolithic board would be 230 mm wide or need two rows of
   receptacles on one board.

Everything else (CM5, hubs, bucks, M.2, UI) routes.

## Option 1: stay monolithic, 4 bays

What we have, plus D8 (0.3 mm vias on the QFN pins, running now). 8 bays would
mean either a 230 mm-wide board or two rows of receptacles with the bridges
squeezed between them: worse routing than today.

## Option 2: bay cards on card-edge slots (recommended if 8 ports matter)

Split the design into a **brain** and identical **bay cards**.

**Brain** (about 140 × 100 mm): CM5, two USB5744 hubs with all four downstream
ports each enabled (that is exactly 8 ports; today ports 3 and 4 are strapped
off), 12 V input, bucks, M.2 bay 5, DIP, OLED, LEDs, and **eight card-edge
slots** along the rear edge. The slot is a standard PCI Express x1 socket (36
contacts, 1.0 mm pitch, about $1, public drawings from Amphenol, TE, Molex),
used with our own pinout, not PCIe signalling: one USB 3 TX pair, one RX pair,
USB 2 D+/D−, 4 × 12 V, 4 × 5 V, 10 × GND, BAY_EN, card-present, LED. Slots on a
14.5 mm pitch: 8 × 14.5 = 116 mm of rear edge.

**Bay card** (about 30 × 45 mm, one design built 8 times): ASM1153E, crystal
and passives, the bay power switch (two P-FETs, two PTC fuses, gate driver),
the bay LED, and the SATA 22-pin receptacle on its rear edge. The card stands
vertically, its plane perpendicular to the rear wall, so the receptacle points
straight out of the rear wall. Because a 22-pin plug is 28 mm wide and the
cards sit 14.5 mm apart, cards alternate between two receptacle heights (about
10 mm and 22 mm above the brain), and the rear wall gets two rows of four
windows.

Why it helps:

* **Routing.** The brain loses four QFNs, four receptacles and four switch
  blocks; what remains routed at 100 % for the single-ended nets and nearly so
  for planes in every run so far. The hub-to-slot pairs are short and straight
  (hubs sit right behind the slots). The bay card is a one-QFN board that the
  router finishes in minutes, and it is routed once.
* **Expansion.** Populate 4 slots or 8. The software already identifies a bay by
  its USB port path, so an 8-bay unit is a config change plus the stagger.
* **Service.** A dead bridge or a torn receptacle is a $6 card, not a new brain.
* **Cost.** One extra connector per bay (about $1) and a small 4-layer card
  panel; the brain shrinks.

What it costs:

* **Height.** A vertical card of about 35 mm plus the slot makes the box about
  50–55 mm tall (today 32 mm). Footprint about 155 × 120 mm.
* **Bandwidth at 8 bays.** Two 5 Gb/s uplinks are shared: with eight drives
  running at once each sees about 100 MB/s, roughly half a modern 3.5" drive's
  speed. Four at once are unaffected.
* **Power at 8 bays.** Eight spinning 3.5" drives draw about 9 A at 12 V with
  the 5 V converted from 12 V; the 120 W brick becomes a 150–180 W brick and
  the input fuse, DIN rating (7.5 A per pin, so two pins in parallel for 12 V)
  and the 12 V bulk caps get revisited. Spin-up stays staggered.
* **Work.** A second KiCad project for the card, the slot pinout, a brain
  re-layout, a new enclosure with the two-row rear wall; then the same routing
  pipeline. About a day of generator work before the router runs.

## Option 3: the bridge at the drive end (rejected for now)

Put the bridge on a tiny board that clips onto the drive and run a single
USB-C cable back to the brain. 3.5" drives need 12 V, which over USB-C means
USB Power Delivery negotiation on every port (a PD controller per port) or a
non-standard cable. Elegant, but it turns a cable into a product; parked.

## Option 4: a hub-and-four-bridges mezzanine

One plug-in module carrying a hub and four bridges on board-to-board
connectors. Moves the hard routing onto the mezzanine without simplifying it;
no expansion story. Not recommended.

## Recommendation

If 8 ports are wanted, option 2. If 4 ports are enough, stay monolithic with
D8 and hand the pair matching to a designer (D7 a).

## Cost (estimate, JLCPCB-class pricing, same basis as FABRICATION.md)

| | Monolithic, 4 bays | Bay cards, 4 populated | Bay cards, 8 populated |
|---|---|---|---|
| Brain board + assembly, first run (2 units + 3 bare) | 290–450 | 280–430 | 280–430 |
| Bay cards: panel, parts, assembly, second assembly setup | — | 90–140 | 160–240 |
| Card-edge slots (about $1 each) | — | 8–12 | 16–24 |
| Per finished unit on top (CM5, cooler, OLED, cables, brick, print) | 120–150 | 125–155 | 175–215 |
| **First two working units, all in** | **530–750** | **640–890** | **870–1150** |
| **Per unit** | **265–375** | **320–445** | **435–575** |

The architecture itself costs a second assembly setup (about $40–70 once) plus
about $15 per unit (card PCB share and slots); the bridge, switch, receptacle
and passives cost the same on a card as on the brain. Eight bays add the bays'
own cost: four cards at about $16 each with parts, four cables, a 150–180 W
brick, heavier input fuse and bulk caps, a taller print. ENIG card fingers are
fine for a few insertions and cost nothing extra; hard gold would add $30–60 per
order.
